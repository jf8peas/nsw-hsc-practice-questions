## Definitions

- **Globalisation:** The increasing integration of economies through flows of trade, finance and investment, technology, labour and information.
- **Economic growth vs development:** Growth: an increase in real GDP. Development: improvement in living standards (income, health, education, environment, equity), measured by e.g. the HDI.
- **Income inequality:** How unequally income is shared across households, measured by the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality) and shown by the Lorenz curve.
- **Absolute vs relative poverty:** Absolute: below a fixed minimum (e.g. $3.00 a day). Relative: well below the typical income in that society.
- **Environmental sustainability:** Meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- **Negative externality:** A cost (e.g. pollution) borne by third parties and not in the price, causing market failure.
- **Comparative advantage:** Producing at a lower opportunity cost than others: China in labour-intensive manufacturing; Nepal in labour, tourism and hydropower.
- **Remittances:** Money sent home by citizens working abroad; a financial flow that boosts consumption (C) and AD.
- **FDI:** Foreign direct investment: a lasting ownership stake by a foreign firm in a local business.

## Theory

- **AD/AS:** Higher exports (X) and investment (I) shift AD right; FDI adds capacity, shifting AS right; result: higher real GDP (growth). Axes: Price level and Real GDP. AD = C + I + G + X − M.
- **Lorenz curve / Gini:** Use for inequality: as inequality rises (China) the curve moves away from the line of equality; as it falls (Nepal) it moves closer.
- **Kuznets curve:** Inequality tends to rise in early industrialisation and fall later; the environmental version applies the same idea to pollution.
- **Harrod-Domar:** Growth depends on savings and investment: low investment and FDI limit Nepal's growth.
- **PPF:** FDI and technology transfer shift the production possibility frontier outward.

## Common errors (good distractors and marking notes)

- The Belt and Road Initiative is international. The rural program is targeted poverty alleviation.
- Made in China 2025 was issued in 2015.
- Never compare China's NBS Gini (about 0.47) with the World Bank Gini (36.0): they're on different scales.
- Nepal's LDC graduation is now 2029, not 2026.
- Nepal's 4,120 MW is all generation capacity, not just hydro.
- Write the year with every figure. If unsure of an exact number, round it ('about a third'); never invent one.

## Context and impact

One line per fact, keyed by fact ID: the context and impact of the fact (its 'so what'). Use these lines for the significance part of an answer and for explanations. They are interpretation, not data: they contain no digits, and no figure, date, name or source may be taken from them. Where a line and a fact row seem to differ, the fact row governs.

- **C01** Ended China's closed, centrally planned economy and began its integration into world markets: the starting point for every globalisation impact in the case study.
- **C02** Special incentives drew foreign investment and export factories to the coast. This drove growth, but concentrated it in coastal cities, a root of the urban–rural gap.
- **C03** Lower tariffs and secure access to world markets turned China into a manufacturing base for the world. Exports and FDI surged, but so did emissions and competition for domestic firms.
- **C04** Builds trade links and infrastructure in other countries, opening markets for Chinese firms and extending China's influence. Use it for international-organisation and other-government questions, never for rural poverty.
- **C05** Aims to move China from cheap assembly to high-tech goods, so it keeps growing as wages rise and relies less on foreign technology.
- **C06** Decades of very rapid growth multiplied incomes and paid for poverty reduction, but at a heavy environmental cost.
- **C07** Growth has roughly halved from its long-run average as the economy matures, though it is still strong for an economy of China's size.
- **C08** Average income rose more than forty-fold in current US dollars, moving China from a low-income to an upper-middle-income economy.
- **C09** Shows deeper integration into the global economy, but also greater exposure to global downturns and trade disputes.
- **C10** A large surplus shows growth still leans on foreign demand. Net exports add to AD, but they expose China to trade tensions.
- **C11** Foreign firms brought capital, technology and management skills, raising productivity and shifting the PPF outward.
- **C12** Foreign investment, once a main driver of growth, is no longer rising. This limits how far integration will keep driving growth.
- **C13** Gains in health, education and income, not just GDP: evidence that growth turned into development.
- **C14** The biggest development gain linked to globalisation, and the key counterpoint to any argument that globalisation only harmed China.
- **C15** On the World Bank's international measure, extreme poverty went from the majority of the population to almost none.
- **C16** Marks the end of extreme poverty in China, reached partly through targeted government programs rather than market forces alone.
- **C17** Many people are still poor by the standard of an upper-middle-income country, so development is incomplete.
- **C18** Moving from farms to cities supplied factory labour and raised incomes, but left a large gap between urban and rural living standards.
- **C19** A shift from broad regional aid to finding and helping each poor household.
- **C20** Shows the government had to act to spread globalisation's gains to rural areas the export boom had left behind.
- **C21** Aimed at lasting income rather than one-off handouts, so families stay out of poverty.
- **C22** Moved people from remote areas with few jobs to places with services and work, though at a high cost and with disruption to communities.
- **C23** The incomes of the poorest rural households roughly doubled in seven years.
- **C24** Growth was uneven: coastal, urban and skilled workers gained most. Pair it with the Lorenz curve moving away from the line of equality.
- **C25** China's own measure also shows high inequality. Quote it on its own, never next to the World Bank figure.
- **C26** Urban households earn more than twice as much as rural ones. The gap narrowed slightly in the latest year but remains large.
- **C27** Rapid, energy-hungry industrial growth, boosted by exports, made China the world's largest emitter: a negative externality of globalisation.
- **C28** Emissions rose with living standards, not just population: the environmental cost of rising incomes.
- **C29** Commits China to cutting emissions while still growing, a test of whether growth and sustainability can be balanced.
- **C30** Puts a price on carbon so polluters pay for the externality. It started with the power sector.
- **C31** Evidence the shift has begun, but most energy use still comes from other sources.
- **C32** Tariffs raise the price of Chinese goods in the US, cutting demand for China's exports and adding uncertainty for the firms that rely on them.
- **C33** Shows how far protectionism against China has risen, a key risk to export-led growth. The truce shows the two economies still depend on each other.
- **C34** Strict lockdowns cut output and incomes, showing growth can be hit by domestic policy shocks as well as global ones.
- **C35** Falling property investment weakens a major source of growth and household wealth, slowing the economy.
- **C36** Fewer workers and more retirees will slow growth and raise pressure on pensions and health spending: a long-term limit on development.
- **N01** Began opening a heavily regulated economy to markets and trade.
- **N02** Opened industries to private and foreign investors, but FDI stayed low, so the impact was limited.
- **N03** Locked in lower tariffs and gave access to world markets, but Nepal had few competitive exports to sell.
- **N04** A huge shock for a small economy: it destroyed homes and infrastructure, cut growth and left Nepal reliant on foreign aid to rebuild.
- **N05** Shows how much a developing economy depends on aid and international organisations in a crisis.
- **N06** Foreign (Indian) investment in hydropower, the main area where FDI and exports could grow together.
- **N07** Balance-of-payments support tied to reform conditions. It helps stability but limits Nepal's policy independence.
- **N08** Could turn electricity into a major export and narrow the trade deficit, but it ties Nepal to a single buyer.
- **N09** Keeps duty-free market access and concessional support for longer, but shows Nepal is not yet ready to compete without them.
- **N10** Average income rose about eightfold in current US dollars but remains low, so Nepal is still a developing economy.
- **N11** Moderate growth, much of it driven by remittance-funded spending rather than investment or exports.
- **N12** Nepal is open to trade, but mostly as an importer.
- **N13** Imports far exceed exports, and remittances help finance the gap: a sign of a narrow export base.
- **N14** Exports pay for only a small fraction of imports, which shows Nepal lacks competitive export industries.
- **N15** Nepal's main link to the global economy is exporting labour. Remittances support household spending and cut poverty.
- **N16** Rising fast and large enough to drive spending and keep the external accounts stable.
- **N17** The key limitation: remittances pay for consumption, not jobs and industry at home, so growth is narrow and fragile.
- **N18** Tiny compared with China. Low foreign investment means little capital, technology and job creation from abroad.
- **N19** Development improved strongly despite modest growth, helped by remittances.
- **N20** Poverty roughly halved in under three decades: the clearest evidence that Nepal's integration, especially through remittances, improved living standards.
- **N21** On the World Bank's international measure, extreme poverty is almost gone.
- **N22** More than half the population is still poor by a higher standard, so development has a long way to go.
- **N23** Unlike in China, inequality fell, because remittances flow straight to ordinary households, including poor rural ones.
- **N24** Tourism is a key source of foreign exchange and jobs, but it is highly vulnerable to global shocks such as the pandemic.
- **N25** Fast growth in power generation is creating a surplus to export.
- **N26** Electricity is becoming Nepal's most promising export: it earns foreign exchange and supplies clean energy to the region.
- **N27** A decade of conflict destroyed infrastructure, deterred investment and disrupted development.
- **N28** Shows the danger of relying on one neighbour for trade: the blockade came on top of the earthquake and almost stopped growth.
- **N29** Being landlocked makes trade costly and leaves Nepal exposed to decisions made in India.
- **N30** The pandemic hit tourism and economic activity, showing how exposed Nepal is to global shocks.
- **N31** Political crises and weak governance deter investment and slow reform, one reason Nepal's strategies have delivered less than hoped.
